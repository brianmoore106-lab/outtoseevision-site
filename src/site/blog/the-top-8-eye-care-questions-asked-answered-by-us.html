1. Is it better to see an optometrist or an ophthalmologist?

For almost everything you'll need in a normal year — it's an optometrist. Here's the difference in one breath: an optometrist (OD) handles comprehensive eye exams, glasses and contact lens prescriptions, and medical eye care like dry eye, infections, and red eyes. An ophthalmologist (MD) is a medical doctor who also performs surgery — cataracts, LASIK, retinal procedures.

 Think of it like your primary care doctor versus a surgeon. You don't book a cardiac surgeon for a physical. If your comprehensive exam turns up something surgical, a good optometrist refers you to a trusted ophthalmologist on Oʻahu and coordinates your care — that's exactly what we do at Out to See.

4. Do I need a referral to see an optometrist?

 No. This one surprises people constantly: you can book an optometrist directly, today, without asking anyone's permission. No referral from your primary care doctor, no insurance gatekeeping for routine exams. If you can book a haircut, you can book an eye exam.

 5. Can an eye exam detect a stroke — or other health problems?

 Yes, and this is the part of our job people underestimate. Your retina is the only place in the body where we can directly watch blood vessels without cutting anything open. A comprehensive exam with retinal imaging can reveal signs of high blood pressure, diabetes, elevated stroke risk, autoimmune conditions, and even certain tumors — sometimes before you have any other symptoms.

 That's why I show every patient the actual photos of their retina and walk through what we're seeing. It's not just "can you read line four" — it's a health check through a window most people never look through.

7. What actually works for dry eye in Hawaii?

Trade winds, salt air, sun, and screens — Kailua is basically a dry-eye laboratory. Drugstore drops may help; they don't always fix the underlying problem. The treatments that actually work depend on which kind of dry eye you have (yes, there's more than one), which is why we start with a proper dry eye evaluation and build a plan around the cause — not just the symptom. If your eyes are burning, watery, or tired by afternoon, that's not "just getting older." It's treatable.
