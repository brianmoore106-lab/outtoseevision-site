1. How often should I actually get an eye exam?

For most healthy adults, every one to two years. The honest answer is that it depends on you.

A rough guide:

If you wear glasses or contacts, once a year. Prescriptions drift slowly, and small changes add up to eye strain and headaches well before you consciously notice any blur.

If you're a young adult with no correction and no concerns, every two years is usually fine.

Once you hit 40, make it yearly. Vision tends to start shifting around then, and the risk of age-related eye conditions goes up.

Kids and teens should come yearly, especially while their eyes are still developing and school is demanding a lot of them.

If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of eye disease, at least once a year, sometimes more.

Forget the calendar if something feels off. Sudden vision changes, new headaches, trouble with night driving, or glasses that just don't feel right anymore are all reasons to come in now rather than waiting for the anniversary.

2. Do I really need an exam if my vision seems fine?

Yes, and this is the one we wish more people knew.

A comprehensive eye exam isn't just about reading the chart. It's one of the only times a doctor gets a clear, direct look at the blood vessels and nerves in your body without making a single incision. That means we can catch early signs of diabetes, high blood pressure, high cholesterol, and eye diseases like glaucoma, often before you feel anything at all.

Most of the conditions that cause permanent vision loss are silent early on. Catching them early is the whole point. "My vision feels fine" is exactly the situation where a routine exam earns its keep.

3. What's the difference between an optometrist and an ophthalmologist?

People mix these up constantly. Here's the quick version.

An optometrist (OD), which is Dr. Mordue, handles your day-to-day eye health: comprehensive exams, glasses and contact lens prescriptions, and diagnosing and managing many eye conditions.

An ophthalmologist (MD) is a medical doctor who specializes in eye conditions and also performs surgery, like cataract removal or LASIK.

For most people, an optometrist is both your first stop and your regular eye care home. If you ever need surgical or specialized care, we coordinate directly with an ophthalmologist we trust, so you're not left to figure it out on your own.

5. Single vision, progressives, or readers. What do I need?

This question tends to show up right around someone's 40th birthday, when restaurant menus mysteriously start requiring longer arms. That's presbyopia, a normal age-related change where the lens inside your eye gets less flexible and close-up focus becomes harder. It happens to essentially everyone eventually.

Your options, in plain terms:

Single-vision lenses correct one distance. Good if you only need help far away, or only up close.

Readers are simple magnifiers for close work, if that's your only need.

Progressive lenses blend several focusing distances into one lens with no visible line, so you can read, work at a computer, and see across the room without swapping glasses.

There's no universally best choice. It comes down to how you use your eyes all day, which is exactly what we talk through during your fitting.

6. Do I really need prescription sunglasses in Hawaii?

We may be a little biased living here, but the sun on this island is no joke. UV protection isn't a luxury add-on, it's eye health.

Cumulative UV exposure is linked to cataracts and other long-term damage, and we get a lot of sun year-round. If you wear a prescription, prescription sunglasses mean you never have to choose between seeing clearly and protecting your eyes. And if you spend time on the water, polarized lenses cut the glare coming off the ocean dramatically.

We carry sunglasses, prescription and non-prescription, from lines we genuinely stand behind, including Maui Jim, Tom Ford, Krewe, and Moscot, chosen with Hawaii's light and climate in mind.

7. How often do kids need eye exams?

Yearly, and starting earlier than most parents expect. Vision problems in children often go unnoticed because kids have no idea their sight isn't normal. They simply adapt, and that quiet adaptation can show up later as trouble with reading, learning, and behavior.

We also offer myopia management, which matters most for kids whose prescription climbs a little more every year. There are real tools now to slow that progression down, and the earlier we start, the more we can do.
